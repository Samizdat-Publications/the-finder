# Player Review

## Gut reaction
This is catnip for space nerds. It feels like an interactive museum exhibit built by someone who deeply loves the source material. It's thrilling to manually decode the Voyager record, line by line, buzz by buzz, and see the logic of the cover actually work. However, right now it plays more like a piece of educational software than a puzzle game; I'm just following explicit instructions rather than making deductions.

## Top problems (ranked, max 7)
1. [hurts] It's a guided tour, not a game. Being told exactly what number to dial in (00:46, "Set Time for one turn to 4.1331 beats") removes all agency. I want to deduce that number from the cover's base-2 math myself.
2. [hurts] Visual clutter. Screens like the Uranium Clock (01:39) have too many separate panels, text boxes, and readouts. My eyes don't know where to rest. Group related information better or reveal it progressively.
3. [nitpick] The "Propose" button mechanic (01:08) feels like a generic web-form submit button rather than a diegetic part of this alien interface. 
4. [nitpick] The data buzz sound (00:43) is authentic but grating. Consider ducking its volume slightly when a player is reading text for a long time.

## Working, don't touch
- The mechanical accuracy of decoding the images (the raster scan, the RGB stacking).
- The 3D pulsar map and the process of matching lines to stars.
- The emotional pivot at 02:59 from the cold, alien analytical perspective to the warm, human one.

## Steam review one-liner
An incredibly detailed, interactive love letter to the Voyager Golden Record that currently holds your hand a little too tightly.

## What I want next
A mode where the "YOUR TURN" box is disabled, and I have to rely solely on the Codex and the cover markings to figure out the inputs myself.

## What is clear
- What I am supposed to click or drag next is always obvious thanks to the green "YOUR TURN" box and highlighted UI elements (e.g., 00:34, clicking the strokes).
- The relationship between the physical record grooves, the needle position, and the resulting decoded data (00:43).

## What confuses
- The sheer volume of text on some screens (01:39). It's hard to parse at a glance which text is lore, which is instruction, and which is scientific explanation without reading it all.
- The pacing of the reveals. Sometimes I am told the answer before I've even had a chance to look at the puzzle.

## What is beautiful
- The 3D pulsar map (01:57) is a standout visualization, especially how the lines snap to the stars.
- The moment the three black and white images combine into a full-color photo of Earth (01:21).
- The transition to the golden, fully lit record when returning to "our time" (02:59).

## What is boring
- Reading the long paragraphs in the "THE REAL STORY" boxes. They are interesting context, but they break the flow of the interactive elements.
- Dialing in exact numbers that the game just handed to me in bold text (00:46).

## Where the sound helps, and where it hurts
- **Helps:** The harsh, rhythmic buzzing of the image data (00:43) grounds the experience in analog reality. It makes the machine feel real. The sudden switch to beautiful acoustic music (Cavatina) at 02:59 is a fantastic, earned emotional reward.
- **Hurts:** The data buzz is loud and abrasive; if I get stuck reading a panel while it's playing, I will absolutely mute the tab.

## The three changes that would most improve it
1. **Remove the training wheels:** Offer a mode that strips out the explicit instructions in the "YOUR TURN" box, forcing the player to actually deduce the settings from the visual clues.
2. **Declutter the UI:** Consolidate the numerous small text panels into larger, tabbed, or progressively revealed sections to reduce cognitive load.
3. **Pacing of information:** Let the player play with the dials and fail *before* presenting them with the "THE REAL STORY" text explaining how it works. Let the interaction lead the learning.
