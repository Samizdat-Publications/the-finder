# Player Review

## Gut reaction
This is like *The Witness* meets a high-end NASA interactive exhibit. The core premise, playing an alien who has to use the hydrogen atom as a universal yardstick to decode the Voyager Golden Record, is absolutely staggering. The actual mechanical process of tuning raw audio frequencies until they snap into a visual image gave me chills. But right now, it feels a bit too much like I'm taking a very cool online astrophysics course rather than playing a game; the interface is incredibly human and web-like, and the text often hands you the answers before you can figure them out yourself.

## Top problems (ranked, max 7)
1. [ruins it] **The UI aesthetic is too human.** (Throughout) The interface looks like a slick React web app. If I am an alien, why am I clicking standard rounded-corner buttons with glowing green hover states? It shatters the roleplay.
2. [hurts] **Over-explanation kills the epiphany.** (e.g., 07:14) When the circle appears squashed, the text immediately tells me *why* and exactly how to fix the aspect ratio. Let me wonder why it's an oval, let me play with the dials, and let me have the realization myself.
3. [hurts] **The "Your Turn" hand-holding.** (e.g., 03:10) The prompts are too prescriptive ("Drag the slider until..."). It feels like a tutorial that never ends. 
4. [hurts] **Text density.** (e.g., 01:22, 10:24) The "What we just learned" panels are massive walls of text. It stops the momentum of the game dead to read a Wikipedia-style entry. 
5. [nitpick] **UI sound effects clash with the atmosphere.** The little digital "bloop" sounds when clicking tabs or confirming things feel cheap compared to the incredible diegetic audio of the record itself.
6. [nitpick] **The "Do it for me" button.** (Throughout) Having a button that just solves the puzzle sitting right there on the screen removes the friction that makes puzzle games rewarding. Hide this in a menu if it's for accessibility.

## Working, don't touch
- The actual puzzle mechanics of decoding the data. Using the spin-flip of hydrogen to establish base units of time and length, and then applying that to the record's RPM and image raster lines, is brilliant.
- The audio design of the record itself. The raw, abrasive noise of the image data makes the technology feel physical and real.
- The visual feedback when tuning the image lines (06:40). Watching the diagonal static slowly straighten out into a coherent picture is incredibly satisfying.

## Steam review one-liner
A breathtakingly nerdy love letter to the Voyager mission that makes you feel like the smartest alien in the galaxy, provided you don't mind reading a textbook to get there.

## What I want next
I want the training wheels taken off. Give me the raw data, the tools to manipulate it, and let me make the connections without a text box telling me what I just did.

## What is clear
The progression of logic is crystal clear. Starting from the universal constant of the hydrogen atom (02:10) and mathematically building up to the record's RPM (03:10) and the image decoding timings (06:40) makes perfect sense. The cause-and-effect of moving a slider and seeing the data react is immediate and understandable.

## What confuses
The sheer amount of information thrown at the player in the summary panels (like at 10:24). When a puzzle is solved, the screen fills with text explaining the real-world history, the math, and the next steps all at once. It's hard to know what is flavor text and what is a crucial clue for the next puzzle.

## What is beautiful
The sequence from 06:40 to 08:30 is a masterpiece. Hearing the terrifying, abrasive screech of the raw data, using the dials to align the raster lines, seeing the calibration circle snap into a perfect sphere, and then watching the first photographs of Earth slowly develop on the screen. It perfectly captures the profound isolation and reach of the Voyager mission.

## What is boring
Reading the "What we just learned" and "The Real Story" boxes. While the history is interesting, stopping the gameplay every two minutes to read a dense paragraph of text (e.g., 01:22 about the RTGs, 10:24 about the numbers) completely halts the pacing and sense of discovery.

## Where the sound helps, and where it hurts
**Helps:** The diegetic sound is phenomenal. The heavy *thunk* of the needle dropping (04:49), the faint hiss of the empty groove, and the harsh, alien screech of the image data make the record feel like a physical, ancient artifact. 
**Hurts:** The UI sound effects. The constant high-pitched digital *blips* and *bloops* when clicking buttons or confirming actions feel like they belong in a mobile gacha game, completely undermining the lonely, analog atmosphere of deep space.

## The three changes that would most improve it
1. **Diegetic UI:** Strip away the web-app aesthetic (the green borders, the standard buttons) and redesign the interface to look like an abstract, alien diagnostic tool or a physical workstation.
2. **Show, Don't Tell:** Cut the explanatory text by 70%. Remove the "Your Turn" instructions. Let the player figure out that they need to adjust the slider to make the circle round, rather than telling them exactly how to do it.
3. **Move history to a Codex:** Take all "The Real Story" historical facts and put them in a separate, optional Codex or journal (I see a Codex button in the top right, use it exclusively for this). Keep the main puzzle screen focused purely on the immediate data and the mystery.
